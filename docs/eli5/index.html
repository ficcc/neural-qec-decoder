How do you fix a computer that makes mistakes all day long?

Quantum computers are made of tiny, delicate parts that go wrong constantly. This page lets you play with the problem using smoke alarms, then shows what a real research project measured.

1. The alarm building

Picture a row of delicate parts (qubits). Any of them can suddenly flip, like a light switch jumping on its own. Between every pair of neighbours there is a smoke alarm that rings when the two neighbours disagree. Nobody is allowed to look at the parts themselves; only the alarms are visible.

What the detective is weighing

Only two stories can explain any set of alarms: one and its mirror image. The detective picks the one with fewer flipped parts (orange).

The detective looks only at the alarms and picks the fewest-mistakes story that explains them. That is the idea behind matching, the classic decoder. A row of 5 parts can always be fixed if at most 2 flipped.

Is a bigger building safer?

Toy model

Bar = how often the detective got it wrong (the tick marks the exact mathematical answer). If mistakes are rare enough, bigger rows are much safer, because more parts must go wrong at once. In the real work, the size of the building is called the distance, d.

2. What if the detective has the wrong map?

Matching needs to know how shaky each part is. A detective with a factory map assumes every part is equally shaky. Real machines are uneven. Here, some parts are shakier than others. A third detective redraws the map just by watching which alarms ring together, with no peeking at the parts.

Toy model7 parts. Bars above them show how shaky each really is.
Machine

In the real experiments (full surface codes with uneven parts) this gap was small: at distance 5 the factory-map detective was only about 10% worse than the one with the true map, and redrawing the map from the alarms alone recovered essentially all of it. The "Very uneven" preset above is deliberately extreme.

3. The student who learns from examples

What if the detective had no map at all, and just practised? A neural network is a learner like that: it sees examples ("these alarms rang, and this is what had really happened") and gets better. The toy student below simply remembers, for every alarm pattern it has seen, which answer was right. For a pattern it has never seen, it has to guess.

Toy model9 parts, each flips 20% of the time.

The map-redrawing detective is already good after a few hundred examples. The student needs thousands. In the real experiments the neural network needed about 100 times more practice than the detective that redraws its map (a million examples against ten thousand). Section 5 shows the real numbers.

4. A better job for the student: the helper

Instead of replacing the detective, the student gets a smaller job: tidy the obvious mess first, then hand what is left to the classic detective. What counts as obvious? Two neighbouring alarms ring and nobody else nearby does: that is clearly one small mistake right between them.

AnimationGreen: obvious pairs the helper can clear. Orange: a tangle the classic detective must untangle.

Press the button to watch the helper work.

There is one dial: how sure must the helper be before it clears something? Too eager and it makes wrong calls; too picky and it does little. Here is what we actually measured in the biggest building we tested (distance 7), using a helper that had only ever practised on smaller buildings.

A ratio below 100% means the combination makes fewer mistakes than the classic detective alone. Clearing too much (a very eager helper) makes more.

Real measurementsThe confirmation on fresh puzzles (distance 7, error rate chosen above)

The "too eager" setting (0.7) failed our pre-stated test at distance 7. A pickier setting (0.95) was added before looking at the fresh puzzles, and it passed: about 2% fewer mistakes at the quietest error rate while still clearing 63% of the alarms.

6. What we are not sure about

  • Everything here is simulated, up to a building of distance 7. Nothing was run on a real quantum computer.
  • The student failing in the big building is about this student and this amount of practice, not about learning programs in general.
  • The helper, as written, is a research program and runs roughly 23 to 31 times slower than the classic detective. Real machines need answers in about a millionth of a second.
  • The "pickier" setting was tested at one value, and the idea came from earlier results. We said so and tested it again on fresh puzzles with the rules written down first.
  • The toy models on this page are illustrations. The numbers in Sections 4 and 5 are the real measurements.

If you remember three things

  1. A detective with a wrong map is not as bad as feared: the classic detective can redraw its map just by watching.
  2. A learning program works best as a helper, not a replacement: clear the obvious mess, leave the hard part to the classic detective.
  3. How sure the helper must be matters a lot, and the right level changes with the size of the machine.
